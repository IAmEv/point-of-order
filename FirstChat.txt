---

## Part 1: Distilling What Makes a Good Chair

Before touching code or mechanics, you need to be ruthless about what skills you're actually simulating. A good collegiate Chair does roughly these things, and each one is a potential game system:

| Chair Skill | Game System It Becomes |
|---|---|
| **Procedural fluency** (Robert's Rules / parliamentary procedure) | Quick-decision prompts, motion routing puzzles |
| **Time management** (keeping committee on pace toward a resolution) | A visible session clock + "committee progress" meter |
| **Reading the room** (recognizing bloc dynamics, dominant/quiet delegates) | Delegate mood/engagement stats you infer from behavior |
| **Fairness & speaker equity** (rotating the speakers list, avoiding favoritism) | Tracked stats on who's spoken, with penalties for imbalance |
| **Crisis handling** (surprise notes, disruptive delegates, walkouts) | Timed event cards / interrupts |
| **Substantive knowledge** (understanding the topic well enough to guide debate) | Optional "ruling on relevance" mini-decisions |
| **Voice & presence** (calm authority under pressure) | Tone choices in dialogue — firm vs. diplomatic vs. warm |

If you build systems around *all* of these, you have a real simulator. If you build around only 1-2, you have a minigame.

---

## Part 2: The Core Gameplay Loop

I'd structure this as a **committee session = a level**. Here's the loop I'd recommend:

**Session Setup (30 sec)** → briefing on topic, committee size, difficulty, any special conditions ("this committee has three dominant blocs," "the USSR delegate is a known filibusterer," etc.)

**Live Session (8–15 min real time, representing a full committee session)** → the main gameplay. Motions fly in, speakers finish, notes arrive, crises trigger.

**Debrief (30 sec)** → scored on 4-5 axes: Procedural Accuracy, Speaker Equity, Committee Progress, Delegate Satisfaction, Crisis Response. Unlock next session.

**Meta-progression** → chairing progressively harder committees (GA Plenary → specialized ECOSOC → Security Council → Crisis committees), each unlocking new mechanics (unmoderated caucuses, crisis notes, veto powers, etc.).

The key is that the **live session** must feel like *controlled chaos you're actively mastering*, not a menu you're clicking through.

---

## Part 3: Making the Controls Intuitive & Immersive

This is where most simulators fail — they either drown you in menus or oversimplify to the point of tedium. Some specific recommendations:

**1. The gavel is your primary verb.** Clicking/tapping the gavel should be the "commit" action for any decision — ruling on a motion, recognizing a speaker, calling to order. Make it satisfying: sound, screen shake, a subtle particle. This becomes muscle memory.

**2. Motions come in as physical "cards" sliding onto your dais.** Each card shows: delegate name, motion type, parameters (duration, speaking time, topic). You drag them into slots:
   - **Approve** slot (goes to vote / auto-passes if procedural)
   - **Rule out of order** slot (with a reason — wrong precedence, not in order)
   - **Table** slot
   
   This is spatial and tactile, not a dropdown menu. Think *Papers, Please* meets *Reigns*.

**3. The speakers list is a visible queue on the side** — portraits of delegates lined up. You tap to recognize next. Delegates whose "engagement" is dropping visually fidget or lower their placards. This teaches you to *see* the room.

**4. Delegate placards are the ambient information layer.** Raised placard = wants to speak. Color-coded by bloc. A placard raised urgently (shaking) = point of order or personal privilege — you must address immediately or take a penalty.

**5. Time pressure via a "flow" meter, not just a clock.** When you're making good calls quickly, the committee has "momentum." Hesitation, wrong rulings, or ignoring the room drains it. Low flow = delegates start talking over each other, sending frivolous motions, disengaging. This is the pressure system that makes it *feel* like chairing.

**6. Crisis notes slide in from the side as folded papers.** You tap to open, read, and choose a response (2-4 options). Timed. This mirrors the real experience of a page handing you a note mid-session.

**7. Voice/tone system via a simple three-way toggle** before responses: 🗣️ Firm / 🤝 Diplomatic / 😊 Warm. Different delegates and situations respond to different tones. Overusing "Firm" tanks delegate satisfaction; overusing "Warm" tanks your authority meter.

## Part 4: What Makes It *Fun* (Not Just Educational)

Educational games die when they feel like homework. Some design principles to avoid that:

- **Failure should be funny, not punishing.** A committee spiraling out of control — delegates arguing, someone motioning to adjourn every 30 seconds, a bloc walking out — should be entertaining to watch even as you lose. Think *Overcooked* kitchen disasters.
- **Escalating absurdity in later levels.** Chair a committee where one delegate is *clearly* representing a rogue crisis arc. Chair a joint-crisis with two rooms. Chair MUNI (Model UN of the Impossible) where the topic is "Resolved: Time Travel."
- **Named delegates with personalities that recur.** The player learns "oh no, it's Delegate Marchetti again, she always motions for unmods." Personality-driven, not stat-driven.
- **Unlockable gavels, dais decorations, cosmetic flair.** Low-stakes collectibles.
- **A "highlight reel" at the end** of your best rulings, funniest chaos moments. Shareable — this is huge for a niche community game; MUN people will absolutely post their scores on LinkedIn and MUN Discord servers.

## Part 5: How to Actually Build It

For a web-hosted game with this kind of interactivity, here's the honest technical path:

**Recommended stack:**
- **Phaser 3** (JavaScript/TypeScript) or **PixiJS** for the 2D game rendering — both are mature, well-documented, run in any browser, no plugins needed.
- **React** for menus, briefings, debrief screens (anything UI-heavy but not real-time).
- **Vite** as your build tool.
- **Zustand or Redux** for game state.
- Host on **Vercel, Netlify, or itch.io** (itch.io is great for indie games and has a built-in MUN-adjacent audience of student developers).

**Alternative:** If you want to prototype faster and don't mind a heavier runtime, **Godot 4** with its HTML5 export is excellent, and its scene/node system fits this kind of "many independent actors on screen" game very well. Unity's WebGL builds are notoriously bloated for something this lightweight — I'd avoid it here.

**Development order I'd recommend:**

1. **Paper prototype first.** Seriously. Print delegate cards, motion cards, run a mock session with a friend as "the committee." You'll learn more in an afternoon than in two weeks of coding.
2. **Vertical slice:** one committee, five delegates, three motion types, no crises. Get the *feel* of the gavel + motion cards + speakers list right. This is your make-or-break milestone.
3. **Add systems one at a time:** crisis notes → tone system → bloc dynamics → meta-progression.
4. **Playtest with actual MUN people early.** UNC has an active MUN team (I'm guessing you know this given your email) — they'll tell you within 10 minutes what feels authentic and what doesn't.
5. **Art last.** Placeholder rectangles until the mechanics sing. Then commission or make simple, stylized art — a clean, semi-flat "diplomatic" aesthetic (deep blues, gold accents, wood-grain dais) will read better than anything trying to be photorealistic.

## Part 6: The Biggest Risks to Avoid

- **Over-simulating procedure.** Robert's Rules has ~200 motions. You need maybe 15. Cut aggressively.
- **Making it a wall of text.** Every piece of information should have a *visual* representation first, text second.
- **No feedback loop on "why did I lose points?"** The debrief must be specific: "You recognized Delegate Chen 6 times and Delegate Okoye 0 times" — not "Equity: C-."
- **Trying to teach everything.** Pick the 3-4 skills you most want to develop and design ruthlessly around those.
